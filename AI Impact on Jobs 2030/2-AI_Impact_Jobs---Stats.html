AI Impact on Jobs 2030 - Statistical Analysis

Notebook 2 : Statistical Analysis

Following the descriptive exploration of the AI Impact on Jobs 2030 dataset, this notebook delves deeper into statistical analyses to better understand patterns related to automation risk.

We focus on key job characteristics such as salary, experience, education, and AI exposure, and explore their relationships with automation probability. This includes examining distributions, testing statistical assumptions, and applying appropriate analyses to summarize and compare groups.

The main guiding question remains:

“Which types of jobs are most at risk of automation, and how do job characteristics are distributed across different levels of automation risk?”

By rigorously examining these variables, we aim to provide clearer insights into the factors associated with automation vulnerability across occupations.

A Welch ANOVA revealed a highly significant effect of job title on automation probability, F(19,1088.93) = 1609.37, p < .001, confirming substantial differences across occupations after accounting for heteroscedasticity.

Games–Howell post-hoc comparisons indicated a clear occupational stratification of automation risk: highly skilled, knowledge-intensive professions exhibited similarly low automation probabilities, whereas routine, manual, and service-oriented occupations showed significantly higher levels of automation risk.

Together, these findings suggest that exposure to automation is primarily structured by broad occupational task characteristics rather than by individual job titles.

Predictors of Automation Probability

Given the bounded nature of the dependent variable and the strong occupational clustering observed in preliminary analyses, a mixed-effects beta regression was estimated with job title specified as a random intercept. This specification allows individual-level predictors to be assessed net of occupational-level heterogeneity.

A mixed-effects beta regression was estimated with job title specified as a random intercept to account for occupational-level heterogeneity. After controlling for this clustering, none of the individual-level predictors—average salary, years of experience, AI exposure, or technological growth—were statistically significant. Key Takeaways: Statistical Analysis

  • Education does not explain automation risk: Non-parametric tests (Kruskal-Wallis, Dunn) and Chi-Square analyses show no significant differences in Automation Probability or Risk Categories by Education Level. The interaction between AI Exposure and Education is also non-significant, reinforcing the minimal role of formal education.

  • Job title drives differences in automation risk: Welch ANOVA and Games-Howell post-hoc tests reveal substantial variation in Automation Probability across occupations. Highly skilled, knowledge-intensive jobs show low automation risk, whereas routine/manual/service-oriented jobs show high risk.

  • Continuous predictors show negligible effects: Mixed-effects Beta regression with job title as a random intercept indicates that Average Salary, Years of Experience, AI Exposure, and Tech Growth have small, non-significant effects on automation probability, highlighting that individual-level predictors explain little beyond occupational clustering.

  • Automation risk is structured by occupation, not education: The combination of statistical tests confirms that differences in automation exposure are primarily driven by job-level characteristics rather than by individual attributes such as education or experience.
